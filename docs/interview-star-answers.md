# 05 | Behavioural

## Question 1

**Describe a scenario where you led the operating model design for a complex data or Artificial Intelligence transformation across multiple business units. How did you structure the engagement?**

### Answer

**Step 1 — Why Investment Committee memoranda matter**  
I will start with why this work matters, because that is how I open every impact engagement.

Before money is committed or a portfolio recommendation is locked, Investments prepares an Investment Committee memorandum. That pack is the evidence trail: what we read, what we believe, what we recommend, and what we want the committee to decide. If it is thin, late, or poorly sourced, the committee delays, asks clarifying questions, or decides with incomplete challenge. So the quality and speed of that pack sit on the critical path of how this agency invests.

I learned that lesson as Artificial Intelligence Operating Model Impact Lead at UBS Wealth Management on the Investment Office and Regional Investment Committee pack. The names change at Future Fund. The critical-path logic does not. When I speak with Alex, Zach, or Matt about Investments, this is the concrete workflow I mean—not “Artificial Intelligence for research” in the abstract.

**Step 2 — What is happening right now**  
I sat with analysts and timed the work. They spent about twelve to fifteen hours on a first draft alone—around **13.2 hours** when we timed it. Most of that was not judgement. It was hunting licensed research and internal notes, copying facts, formatting, and chasing citations. Senior people fixed structure instead of challenging the thesis. Juniors learned slowly because they were stuck in collation. The current operating model was clear and painful: humans do retrieval, drafting, and judgement in one lump, with uneven source trails. Shadow use of consumer chat tools on proprietary research was also starting to appear, which created a real control risk.

**Step 3 — The Artificial Intelligence use case**  
I named the use case precisely: **Artificial Intelligence–assisted Investment Committee memorandum preparation**.

Inside an approved application and data boundary, Artificial Intelligence retrieves from licensed and internal sources, summarises with citations, flags contradictions, and drafts factual sections. The analyst verifies every substantive claim. The portfolio manager still challenges the thesis. Risk keeps its independent path. The Investment Committee still decides. Artificial Intelligence does **not** approve the investment, set conviction, set allocation, or submit trades.

**Step 4 — Future state**  
In future state, the analyst opens a case in the approved tool.  
Artificial Intelligence retrieves only approved sources and returns cited summaries.  
Artificial Intelligence drafts factual sections; unsupported content is labelled.  
The analyst edits, verifies, and attests.  
The portfolio manager challenges independently.  
The existing Risk, secretariat, and Investment Committee path continues.  

Future state is not “the machine decides.” Future state is “the machine accelerates evidence assembly; humans keep investment judgement.”

**Step 5 — What we were trying to save / expedite**  
Draft time: **13.2 hours → about 9 hours**, roughly **25–30 percent**.  
Senior capacity: Research Head and portfolio manager time moves from formatting to challenge.  
Quality cost: fewer last-minute pack defects and missing citations.  
Risk cost avoided: less shadow use of consumer chat tools on proprietary research.  
This was not a headcount-cut business case. It was a capacity and control case.

**Step 6 — Why this is a strong impact-assessment use case**  
It hits process, roles, decision rights, human oversight, second-order effects such as junior learning and committee volume, data sensitivity, technology ownership, and Governance interest—without making Artificial Intelligence the decision maker. It is material enough for Investments and bounded enough to pilot. That is the Impact Lead sweet spot Future Fund describes.

**Step 7 — Ideas I rejected**  

| Idea | Why I rejected or redirected |
|------|------------------------------|
| Paste proprietary research into a consumer chatbot | Data path prohibited; no control, no logging |
| Artificial Intelligence recommends buy/sell and conviction | Makes Artificial Intelligence the investment decision maker |
| Auto-submit trades from memorandum language | Money movement without human authority |
| Five local team bots instead of shared platform | Duplicate ownership; inconsistent controls |
| Auto-write final Investment Committee minutes as authoritative record | Secretariat and legal record risk |

Of about **11** ideas in early triage, I accepted **2** into discovery first: this memorandum assist under Investments, and a secure enterprise knowledge platform under Technology and Infrastructure.

**Step 8 — How I structured the engagement**  
I started with a charter so ownership was clear before discovery spend. I ran weekly triage with named owners mandatory. I did Investments discovery by shadowing real memorandum work, plus a short Technology and Infrastructure discovery on identity, logging, and approved sources. I facilitated a joint design workshop with **8** people for human–Artificial Intelligence boundaries. I wrote the impact assessment with Investments, People and Change, and Risk. On the ownership wall I said this out loud: Technology and Infrastructure owns tool safety; Investments owns the investment outcome; Governance owns residual risk; People and Change owns learning; I own clarity of those lines. I do not own the investment decision.

**Result:** one sequenced first wave—platform controls first, then a bounded Investments pilot—not five parallel Artificial Intelligence projects.

**Step 9 — Example B: the playbook behind that structure**  
I did not invent that engagement design on the morning of the workshop. Over time in the same Impact Lead seat I formed, implemented, and mastered an Artificial Intelligence Operating Model playbook: mobilise and intake, discover and design, impact assessment, assure and decide, pilot and implement, review and learn. The playbook gave me reusable artefacts—current-state maps, decision-right matrices, second-order registers, readiness heat maps, and decision papers. So when the memorandum use case arrived, I applied a method I had already tested and hardened. That is what a late-starting horizontal workstream at Future Fund needs on day one.

**Bridge for Alex, Zach, and Matt**  
Example A is the Investment Committee memorandum operating-model design. Example B is the playbook that makes that design repeatable across Investments, Governance, Technology and Infrastructure, and People and Change. Together they are the Impact Lead job.

## Question 2

**Share an example of identifying second-order or systemic risks when introducing machine learning or Artificial Intelligence into operational workflows.**

### Answer

I will stay with the same Investment Committee memorandum use case, because the first-order pitch was only “go faster,” and that is exactly when second-order risk gets missed.

**Right now.**  
Same Investment Committee memorandum process. Humans retrieve, draft, and judge. The business wanted speed. If I had only measured hours saved, I would have hidden the systemic effects.

**Use case.**  
Assisted drafting and retrieval with citations. Humans remain accountable for content and for the investment recommendation.

**Future state.**  
Faster evidence pack. Mandatory human attestation. Explicit Artificial Intelligence may and may not. Exception queue if citations fail. Kill-switch if controls break.

**What we were trying to save.**  
Analyst hours—about **30 percent** draft-time ambition—and senior review waste, without creating hidden risk that costs more later: wrong committee packs, privilege leaks, or deskilled juniors.

**Why this is good for impact assessment.**  
Second-order risks are the Impact Lead’s job. This use case forces them into the open.

**What I did.**  
I ran a two-hour workshop with **nine** people spanning Investments, Risk, Governance partners, People and Change, and Technology and Infrastructure. I asked four hard questions.

1. If Artificial Intelligence summarises, how do juniors still learn? → **4 hours** minimum raw-source review per memorandum for juniors, logged.  
2. If draft time falls from **13.2** to about **9 hours**, does committee volume explode? → pilot cap **8 analysts / 3 sectors / 6 weeks**.  
3. If a citation is wrong, can the analyst defend the pack? → attestation + **95 percent** citation bar.  
4. If every desk builds a bot, what does Governance inherit? → approved-source registry + standard attestation across the Workstream Lead.

**Human oversight and exception pathways.**  
The analyst can override. Failed citations go to a human-only queue. The Research Head or Product Owner can suspend. Cyber owns access breach response. Artificial Intelligence never becomes the decision maker.

**What I rejected while doing this.**  
Skip attestation to save time. Artificial Intelligence sets conviction. Web-open model on manager letters.

**Pilot.**  
Draft about **9.4 hours**. Citations about **96 percent**. Overrides about **11 percent**. One paraphrase incident caught in week **4**, fixed in **5** business days. No new Artificial Intelligence decision rights.

**Example B link.**  
Those four questions and the oversight pattern are now a standard page in my Artificial Intelligence Operating Model playbook. I do not rediscover second-order risk from scratch on every use case. That is the reusable craft Alex’s workstream needs when Investments moves quickly and Governance still has to sleep at night.

## Question 3

**Facilitate co-design with senior executives who held competing priorities—how did you gain alignment?**

### Answer

I will take you to the fortnight where four Future Fund–style vertical agendas collided, because that is the co-design problem this role is built for.

**Right now.**  
Investments wanted the memorandum pilot live. Risk wanted assurance first. Technology and Infrastructure wanted platform-first. People and Change wanted no silent role redesign. Everyone said they were unblocking Artificial Intelligence. Together they blocked each other.

**Use cases on the table.**  
Memorandum assist. Secure knowledge platform. An informal push for consumer Copilot on research—which I would not accept.

**Future state we were aligning on.**  
Productivity inside approved boundaries: shared platform controls, human decision rights intact, sequenced pilots—not every team inventing its own tool.

**What we were trying to save.**  
Calendar thrash. Delivery rework from conflicting directions. Risk cost of shadow Artificial Intelligence.

**What I rejected in the room.**  
Copilot on proprietary research this month. Artificial Intelligence recommends conviction for speed. Skip People and Change until after the pilot.

**What I did.**  
Monday: **3-page** pre-read; mark agree, disagree, or debate.  
Thursday: **3.5 hours** with **12** people.  
First **30 minutes**: vote non-goals only—no investment decisions by Artificial Intelligence; no proprietary data in unapproved models; business units keep accountability.  
Next: swimlane for who decides pilot, residual risk, model change, expand users, and suspend. I removed myself as residual-risk approver.  
Then: forced rank—Wave A platform, Wave B conditional Investments pilot.  
Same day: decision log with **7** decisions and **2** open disputes.  
Next week: **45-minute** pre-brief, then formal approval with conditions.

**Result.**  
A shared sequence. Not **100 percent** happiness for everyone. That is what good looks like in a lean horizontal seat.

**Example B link.**  
The pre-read, non-goals vote, swimlane, forced rank, and decision log are facilitation patterns I baked into the playbook after repeating the same executive clash more than once. For Alex, Zach, and Matt, that means I can run cross-agency co-design without inventing the workshop design under pressure.

## Question 5

**Manage stakeholders hesitant about changing decision rights / accountabilities.**

### Answer

I will use an Investment Operations example, because hesitation around posting and segregation of duties is rational—and Future Fund will be equally sensitive there.

**Right now.**  
Investment Operations works reconciliation breaks manually under segregation of duties. A vendor demo showed “straight-through Artificial Intelligence resolution.” The operations lead heard one sentence: Artificial Intelligence will post to the ledger. He said no. He was right.

**Use case I shaped.**  
**Artificial Intelligence–assisted break classification and root-cause suggestion**—humans still authorise every financial correction.

**Future state.**  
Break lands. Artificial Intelligence suggests class and cause. Analyst accepts or rejects. Supervisor approves posting under today’s dual-control rules. Artificial Intelligence never posts and never sends settlement instructions.

**What we were trying to save.**  
Time-to-classify. Rework from mis-tags. Overtime on exception queues—without buying audit findings.

**What I rejected.**  
Vendor auto-post and auto-settle. Skip supervisor approval because confidence is high. Pilot without Risk in the room.

**What I did.**  
Half-day mapping their real controls. Rewrite non-goals in their words. Role card making the supervisor still accountable. **90-minute** Risk clinic so Risk said assist-only protects segregation of duties. People and Change **45-minute** “verify then act” for **12** staff. Operations lead presents the non-goals at governance himself.

**Pilot metrics.**  
Time-to-classify, false tags, overrides, and **zero** Artificial Intelligence-initiated posts.

**Example B link.**  
“Assist classification, never post” is now a hard-stop pattern in my playbook triage rules. I do not re-argue segregation of duties from zero every time a vendor arrives. That is how Impact Lead advice stays consistent for Governance and Operations—and how hesitant owners become champions of a safe design.
